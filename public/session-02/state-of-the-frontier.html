How a Predictor Becomes an Assistant

1 · Pretraining
gives: capability

Predict the next token on roughly the internet. Raw knowledge and pattern power — but no idea it should be helpful.

→
2 · Instruction Tuning
gives: format

Trained on examples of following directions. Now it answers questions instead of continuing them.

→
3 · RLHF
gives: manners

Thousands of human raters pick the better answer; the model is optimized to please them. Side effects: sycophancy, verbosity, over-refusal.

Reading a Benchmark Claim — The 5 Red Flags

①
Saturation

Frontier scores cluster above 88–90% — differences up there are statistically meaningless. (MMLU first, GPQA following.)

②
Contamination

Models have reproduced benchmark answers verbatim from training data (documented on SWE-bench Verified) — inflated scores.

③
Harness

Was the score achieved with max reasoning effort, tools, retries, special settings? (GPT-6 Astra's launch numbers drew exactly this criticism.)

④
Relevance

A math-olympiad score says nothing about your invoice processing — enterprises measure a ~37% average gap between lab benchmarks and production performance.

⑤
Freshness

Static benchmarks decay; prefer live, refreshed benchmarks — like LiveBench (refreshed monthly) and, best of all, your own eval on your own task (we build these in Session 11).

Picking a Model — 4 Questions Before Price Sheets

①
Frontier reasoning — or extraction/formatting?

If it's extraction or formatting, the mid-tier is 5–10× cheaper.

②
What's your context need?

A prompt, a document, or a million tokens? Size the window to the job.

③
Latency: chat-speed or batch-overnight?

Interactive needs fast tokens; pipelines can wait — and pay less.

④
What happens when it's wrong?

Is there a human check? No check → you need guardrails before you need capability (Session 10).

Similar accuracy has been observed at 50× cost variation. The savings are in the picking.
